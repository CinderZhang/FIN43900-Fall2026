Start with the most abused number in any forecast: growth. Reported revenue grew sixteen percent in 2024 and four point seven percent in 2025. If you put four point seven percent into a model and compound it, you have already made your first mistake.

Look at the second line. In December 2023 Asbury bought the Koons dealership group. Koons contributed two point eight billion of revenue in 2024. Now the line that matters, highlighted. Two ratios run a car dealer. Gross margin, gross profit over revenue: eighteen point six percent, seventeen point two, seventeen point one. It fell and then stabilised. The company's own discussion tells you why: new-vehicle margins were unusually high in the shortage years and have come back down; that is how the company itself puts it. The second ratio is the one you will not find in a textbook, and the one dealers actually manage: SG&A as a share of gross profit. It answers the question, what does it cost this company to earn a dollar of gross profit? Fifty-eight point seven cents, then sixty-four point one, then sixty-four point seven. That is the whole story of the falling net income. Revenue up, gross margin normal, and the cost of running the stores rose faster than the gross profit they produced. Part of that, how much we cannot measure from the filings, is buying and integrating two large groups in two years. How fast it comes back down is the single biggest judgment in this valuation, and we will say so when we make it.

Now the balance-sheet habits. Inventory days, inventory over cost of sales times three sixty-five: fifty-four, fifty-one, fifty-two. Stable. A dealer carries about fifty-two days of vehicles.

The next line is the one that makes a dealer different from every other company you will model. Dealers do not pay cash for the cars on the lot. They borrow against them, from the manufacturers' finance arms and from banks. Those inventory loans are called floor plan, and they cover eighty-six percent of inventory in 2024 and ninety-five percent in 2025. Two billion dollars of vehicles, funded by lenders who hold the cars as collateral. When inventory rises, the loan rises with it. We will model it exactly that way, and later I will show you what happens if you forget.

Depreciation over property and equipment, roughly two point seven to two point nine percent. Steady.

Now look at the two capital-spending lines together, because this is a trap that catches professionals. The 10-K says capital spending was one forty-two, three oh eight, two oh five million. The standardised field from a data provider says one forty-two, one forty-six, and nineteen. Nineteen million. For a company with three billion dollars of property. The provider's tag picked up the wrong line in 2025. If you had trusted it, your free cash flow would be nearly two hundred million too high. Every number from a data provider gets checked against the filing before it goes anywhere near a model. That check is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

Impairment charges: one seventeen, one fifty, one forty-one. These are write-downs of franchise rights, the right to sell a brand in a territory, which a dealer carries as an asset. Non-cash, and they happen every year. A charge that recurs is not one-off, so we will carry it, but we will remember it is not cash.

Effective tax rate: twenty-four point eight, twenty-five point two, twenty-five point seven percent. That is the twenty-one percent federal rate plus about four points of state tax, drifting up slightly.

That is the history read. Now we turn it into assumptions.

Stop on this screen for a minute, because it answers a question you will be asked in your first week at work: why is this in Python and not in a spreadsheet? Look at what is on the screen. Seven assumptions. Each one has a name you can read, growth, gross margin, tax rate, and next to each one, in the same line, the label and the reason: judgment, history, guidance, and why. In a spreadsheet that number lives in a cell called C7, and the reason lives in your memory, or nowhere.

Four things follow from that, and you will see all four today. First, when you change one number, everything downstream reruns, every statement, every check, the value, and if something breaks the model stops and tells you which year and by how much. A spreadsheet recalculates silently. Second, this model has a test suite: forty-three tests that run in two seconds and prove the arithmetic still ties. Nobody tests a spreadsheet. Third, every change to an assumption is a line in a history you can read: who moved the growth rate, when, and from what. Fourth, and this is the part that changes what the job is: you do not write most of this code. You describe the change in a sentence, your AI partner edits the model, and your job becomes judging whether the assumption is right. The skill that pays is the judgment, not the formula.

And there is a door that opens from here. These same lines, with the assumptions behind a few sliders, become a small application. A colleague moves gross margin, watches the value move, and asks a better question than they would from a PDF. A student who can hand a portfolio manager a working, personalised valuation tool is doing something a spreadsheet builder cannot, and in my experience it is a thing people pay for. You will still be asked for the spreadsheet, and you will export one. But the thinking will have happened here.

Here is the discipline. Every assumption is a named number, set in one place, with a label: history, guidance, or judgment. History means the number is a ratio we just computed and we are carrying it. Guidance means management said it. Judgment means we chose it, and we owe you the reason.

Organic growth, one point eight percent a year. Judgment. The stores already owned grew one point two; the industry grows with the economy at low single digits; we sit a little above the same-store figure and well below the bought four point seven. Acquisitions are excluded; if Asbury buys another group, that is a separate decision with its own price tag.

Gross margin, seventeen point zero five percent, essentially the 2025 margin, held flat. I label that judgment, not history, because holding it flat rather than extrapolating the decline is a choice. The reason for the choice is the one we just read: the shortage premium is gone.

SG&A over gross profit. Judgment, and the big one. Sixty-six and a half percent in 2026, up from sixty-four point seven, because Herb Chambers was bought halfway through 2025 and the first full year of integration costs money. Then sixty-five and a half, then sixty-four and a half for the last three years. A recovery back near the 2024 level, not back to the fifty-nine percent of 2023. If you believe the cost savings from combining the groups come faster, the second video shows you exactly what that is worth.

Depreciation, two point six eight percent of the opening property balance. History. Impairment, one hundred and twenty million a year. Judgment: below the three-year average of one thirty-six, a recurring non-cash charge we neither zero out nor inflate.

Capital spending, two hundred and fifty million a year. Guidance: management's figure for 2026. Carrying it for five years is our judgment. Note the caveat on screen: the first half of 2026 ran hotter, around three thirty-nine annualised. That challenges the assumption; it does not yet replace it.

Tax, twenty-five and a half percent. Judgment: between the three-year average of twenty-five point two and the 2025 rate of twenty-five point seven.

# Opening balance sheet: FY2025 as filed (cash and long-term debt come from the 10-K, not the data provider).
opening = dict(year=2025, revenue=17999.0, inventory=2135.8, floorplan=2027.0, ppe=3070.4,
               other_assets=11618.2-40.4-2135.8-3070.4, other_liabilities=7726.5-2027.0-3572.0,
               debt=3572.0, revolver=0.0, cash=40.4, equity=3891.9-0.2)

def project_year(prev, i):
    """One projected year from the prior year's balance sheet and the assumptions above."""
    y = {'year': prev['year'] + 1}
    # income statement
    y['revenue']      = prev['revenue'] * (1 + growth)
    y['gross_profit'] = y['revenue'] * gross_margin
    cost_of_sales     = y['revenue'] - y['gross_profit']
    y['sga']          = y['gross_profit'] * sga_to_gp[i]
    y['da']           = prev['ppe'] * da_ratio
    y['impairment']   = impairment
    y['operating_income'] = y['gross_profit'] - y['sga'] - y['da'] - y['impairment']
    y['interest_floorplan'] = prev['floorplan'] * rate_floorplan
    y['interest_debt']      = prev['debt'] * rate_debt + prev['revolver'] * rate_revolver
    y['pretax']       = y['operating_income'] - y['interest_floorplan'] - y['interest_debt']
    y['tax']          = max(0.0, y['pretax']) * tax_rate
    y['net_income']   = y['pretax'] - y['tax']
    # balance sheet, everything except cash
    y['inventory']    = cost_of_sales * inventory_days / 365
    y['floorplan']    = y['inventory'] * floorplan_ratio
    y['ppe']          = prev['ppe'] + capex - y['da']
    delta_other_wc    = (y['revenue'] - prev['revenue']) * other_wc_ratio
    y['other_assets'] = prev['other_assets'] + delta_other_wc - y['impairment']
    y['paydown']      = min(debt_paydown, prev['debt'])
    y['debt']         = prev['debt'] - y['paydown']
    y['other_liabilities'] = prev['other_liabilities']
    y['equity']       = prev['equity'] + y['net_income'] - buyback
    # cash flow to equity, then cash as the RESULT
    y['fcfe'] = (y['net_income'] + y['da'] + y['impairment'] - capex
                 - (y['inventory'] - prev['inventory']) - delta_other_wc
                 + (y['floorplan'] - prev['floorplan']) - y['paydown'])
    cash_before = prev['cash'] + y['fcfe'] - buyback
    y['draw']  = min(max(0.0, min_cash - cash_before), max(0.0, revolver_limit - prev['revolver']))
    y['repay'] = min(prev['revolver'], max(0.0, cash_before - min_cash))
    y['revolver'] = prev['revolver'] + y['draw'] - y['repay']
    y['cash']  = cash_before + y['draw'] - y['repay']
    y['total_assets'] = y['cash'] + y['inventory'] + y['ppe'] + y['other_assets']
    y['total_liabilities_equity'] = y['floorplan'] + y['debt'] + y['revolver'] + y['other_liabilities'] + y['equity']
    return y

y26 = project_year(opening, 0)

# The rule, enforced: the model refuses to go on if a balance sheet does not balance or cash breaks the floor.
def assert_balanced(years, tol=0.05):
    for y in years:
        gap = y['total_assets'] - y['total_liabilities_equity']
        if abs(gap) > tol:
