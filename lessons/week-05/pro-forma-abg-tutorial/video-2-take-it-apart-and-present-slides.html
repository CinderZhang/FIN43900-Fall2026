Ten percent was a placeholder. The honest number starts with beta: how much this stock moves when the whole market moves. We take sixty-one month-end prices for Asbury and for the S&P 500, August 2021 to August 2026, turn them into sixty monthly returns, and ask one question: when the market moved one percent, how much did Asbury move, on average?

The answer is the slope of a line through sixty points, and it has a formula you should know by heart: beta equals the covariance of the two return series divided by the variance of the market's returns. Here it is zero point seven one. Read the rest of the grid, because the rest is where the judgment lives. Asbury's monthly volatility is ten point three percent against the market's four point six; the stock moves more than twice as much as the market. But the correlation is only zero point three two, so most of that movement is Asbury's own, not the market's. That is why the beta comes out below one despite the volatility.

The next line: the standard error, the statistical uncertainty of that slope, is zero point two eight. The usual convention is two standard errors either side, which puts the true beta anywhere from about zero point two to one point three. And R-squared is zero point one zero: the market explains ten percent of Asbury's monthly moves. Ninety percent is company and industry.

The last line is the industry's fix for exactly this problem. The Blume adjustment shrinks a measured beta toward one, two-thirds of the estimate plus one-third of one, which gives zero point eight one. Most data vendors quote you the adjusted number without saying so.

One regression on one stock is noisy. The desk fix is to borrow information from the peers. Four public dealer groups: AutoNation, Lithia, Group 1, Penske. Their measured betas run from zero point seven one to one point two two. But each carries its own debt load, and leverage makes equity riskier, so before you average them you strip the leverage out. Unlevered beta is levered beta divided by one plus one minus the tax rate times debt over equity. Average the four unlevered betas: zero point five eight. That is the risk of running car dealerships, before anyone borrows. Then put Asbury's own leverage back: debt over equity of one point zero three, and the relevered beta is one point zero three. Cost of equity, nine point five four percent. Four noisy estimates averaged beat one noisy estimate; that is the whole point.

Two rules on the way through, because people get both wrong. First: the discount rate always wants the levered beta, at the leverage you are forecasting. A regression beta is already levered. Unlevering is only the bridge that lets you move a beta between companies. Second, and specific to this industry: floor plan, the inventory loans from Video 1. We excluded it from debt for every one of these companies, because our model treats it as an operating liability everywhere else. Include it when you unlever and exclude it when you relever and you have manufactured a lower beta and a higher value out of an inconsistency. Same treatment on both legs, or the discount rate lies.

One more screen on the terminal value, because eighty percent of the answer lives there and every line of it is a choice. Our base holds debt flat after 2030: the company refinances what matures, pays interest, and never repays principal. That is what going concerns do. It is also the conservative reading, because a company growing two and a half percent a year with a constant debt ratio would borrow a little more each year, and that borrowing is cash the owners do not have to put in. Borrow a third of each year's reinvestment, which is the Damodaran form: three sixteen. Let debt grow in step with the firm: three twenty-four. Both defensible. We stay flat, and we say so.

The same screen shows the other direction, which for a car dealer is the one the market is thinking about. If the industry stops growing, terminal growth zero: two twenty-nine. If it shrinks one percent a year forever: two twelve. Hold that thought for the price-implied table in a moment.

And one accounting convention, flagged by our reviewers: the model saves thirty-one million of tax a year on the impairment charge, a book write-down. In practice that is usually a deferred-tax item with no cash saving. Take it away and the value is two seventy-one. We keep the settled convention, and we put the twenty-one dollars on the screen so nobody has to take our word for it.

A note on that reinvestment line, because it is the honest caveat about our own growth number. In 2030 the company reinvests one fifty-five out of five twenty-seven of net income, about twenty-nine percent, and we grow the cash flow at two and a half percent forever. That implies an eight and a half percent return on what it retains, below the ten percent cost of equity. In other words our terminal growth destroys a little value at the margin. That is conservative, and it is the kind of thing you disclose rather than hide.

def solve(key, lo, hi, target=PRICE, path=None):
    """Bisection search: halve the interval until the model equals the market price."""
    f = lambda x: value(**{key: (path(x) if path else x)})['per_share'] - target
    for _ in range(60):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if f(lo) * f(mid) <= 0: hi = mid
        else: lo = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

Here is the question that turns a model into an argument. The market says one eighty-three. We say two ninety-two. Instead of asking who is right, ask: what would have to be true for one eighty-three to be exactly right? Take one assumption at a time, hold the other eighteen at base, and search for the value that makes the model equal the price. The search is a bisection: halve the interval until the model lands on one eighty-three.

Cost of equity: thirteen point eight percent. Run that back through the formula and it is a beta of about two, when nothing we measured is above one point three. The cost ratio: six point two points higher than our path, every year, worse than any year the company has posted. Read this the right way. None of these is a forecast, and the truth is probably several of them partly at once. What the table tells you is the size of the disagreement in operating language. The market is not pricing a slightly worse Asbury; it is pricing a dealer that either never recovers its cost ratio or has to spend far more than it says to stand still.
