The worked case — one peer, by hand

EV/EBITDA — enterprise value ÷ earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: what the whole operating business costs per dollar of operating earnings. P/E — equity value ÷ net income: what the shareholders' claim costs per dollar of profit after interest.

Alpha EV/EBITDA = 1,200 / 120 = 10.0× · Alpha P/E = 900 / 75 = 12.0×

The worked case — one peer, by hand
Peer EV/EBITDA P/E
Alpha 10.0× 12.0×
Beta 16.0× 20.0×
Gamma 9.0× 15.0×
Delta 16.0× 15.0×

Compute Beta's P/E yourself before you look at the row — equity value 1,200, net income 60. Never accept a multiple you did not compute — providers disagree on definitions, and Week 1 showed you how badly.

9× to 16× — nearly a factor of two, for companies your own policy called comparable. The dispersion IS information. Either the market is pricing real differences your policy ignored — growth, margins, risk — or a peer does not belong. Both answers are findings. The one thing the spread is not is noise to be averaged away.

Apply the medians — and the trap

Target: EBITDA 80 · net income 40 · cash 50 · debt 300 · 50M diluted shares.

Median EV/EBITDA = median(10.0, 16.0, 9.0, 16.0) = 13.0× → EV = 13.0 × 80 = 1,040 → bridge: 1,040 + 50 − 300 = 790 → 790 / 50 = $15.80

Predict before you read the second line: the P/E route uses a higher multiple, 15×. Does it land above or below $15.80? Commit out loud, then look.

Median P/E = median(12.0, 20.0, 15.0, 15.0) = 15.0× → equity = 15.0 × 40 = 600 → 600 / 50 = $12.00 (no bridge)

Enterprise multiples need the bridge; equity multiples already crossed it. Net income is what is left after the lenders are paid, so a P/E answer is already the shareholders'. Add cash and subtract debt to it and you have counted the capital structure twice — one of the most common junior errors in existence, and one an interviewer can spot in four seconds.

For every candidate return: (a) is it publicly traded today; (b) is it an operating company today, or has it been acquired or taken private; (c) how its business model matches the economics my policy names — revenue model, growth, margin structure, capital intensity; (d) its scale and geography against my target; (e) the data as-of date and the source for every fact you give me. Candidates, not conclusions. The machine's job is to widen the search — never to reopen the criteria after it has seen the answers.

Two tracks at once — 3. Audit, disposition, price

(~16 minutes. Both lists came back while you were learning.)

Run it in this order. A range computed before the audit is a range you cannot defend.

  1. Merge both lists into one candidate table. Duplicates collapse into one row. Where the two systems disagree, keep both — disagreement is search evidence.
  2. Audit every candidate against the frozen policy — still public? still an operating company? business model actually matching the economics your policy names? scale and geography? — and attach a dated source to every load-bearing fact. An AI-supplied fact is a lead, not evidence. Go to the filing.
  3. Disposition each one: accept · qualify · reject — with the source named and the consequence stated.
  4. Compute your own multiples for every peer that survives. Then take the median, and know why it is the median: with a handful of peers, one outlier drags a mean wherever it likes.
  5. Price it — at least one enterprise multiple and at least one equity multiple, into an implied per-share range. The bridge goes on the enterprise route only.
  6. The changed-peer test: remove or qualify one borderline peer, predict what the range does, then run it and interpret the move. Lab 07's own guard: "Do not manufacture a rejection when no candidate is borderline." No rejected candidate is a real result.

The shape, not your answer (one disposition): "Codex proposed Meridian Corp — 'same subscription model, similar scale.' → QUALIFY. Verified against Meridian's FY2025 Form 10-K, filed February 18, 2026: still public, still operating, subscription revenue 71% of total — the model does match. What my policy actually catches is scale: Meridian's revenue is roughly half my target's, and my policy qualifies rather than accepts a peer below two-thirds of target scale. Consequence: Meridian stays in the median, but it is the first peer I drop in the changed-peer test. Source: the 10-K segment table — not the model's summary of it."
